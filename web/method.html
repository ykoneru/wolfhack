How the ratio study is made

Fair Share runs a sales-ratio study on Wake County single-family homes. A sales ratio is the county's assessed value divided by what the home actually sold for. It is the standard way assessors are checked, and the thresholds here come from the International Association of Assessing Officers Standard on Ratio Studies.

Where the numbers come from

Every figure is pulled from a public source. Nothing is modelled or imputed.

  • Assessed values, sale prices, sale dates, year built, heated area, and parcel location come from the Wake County Property/Parcels ArcGIS feature service.
  • Tract boundaries come from Census TIGER/Line.
  • Owner-occupancy, median owner value, and renter cost burden come from American Community Survey tables B25003, B25077, and B25070.

Which sales count

Wake County reassesses on a four-year cycle and the current values took effect on January 1, 2024. The equity measures therefore use 2024 sales only, so the assessed value and the sale price describe the same moment. Mixing later years would conflate market drift with assessment inequity.

A sale is dropped when it is not plausibly arm's length:

  • Price at or under $50,000, which is usually a family transfer or a partial interest.
  • A ratio below 0.4 or above 2.0, which usually means a bundled deed or a parcel that changed after the sale.
  • A year built at or after the sale year, because a home finished after the assessment date was never assessed as the house that sold.

Address search is limited to the 19,004 single-family sales from 2024, so a home's own ratio and every benchmark it is compared against describe the same moment. Of those, 12,799 survive the arm's-length filters and become the official equity measures.

The four measures

Each one answers a different question, and all four are reported whether or not they are flattering.

MeasureWakeStandard
Median ratio — is the overall level right0.9510.90 to 1.10
Coefficient of dispersion — are similar homes treated alike7.615.0 or lower
Price-related differential — do cheap and expensive homes differ1.0160.98 to 1.03
Price-related bias — the same question as a regression slope-0.030-0.05 to 0.05

The coefficient of dispersion is the average distance from the median ratio, as a percentage of the median. The price-related differential is the plain mean ratio divided by the dollar-weighted mean ratio; above 1.03 is called regressive, meaning cheaper homes carry more assessed value per dollar of market price. Price-related bias is the slope of a regression of ratio deviation on home value, and a verdict of regressive is only reported when both of those agree, so one noisy number cannot decide it.

What Wake County actually shows

Wake meets all four thresholds. The assessment level is close to the market, and similar homes are treated consistently.

The price bands still show a steady tilt. Sorted into ten equal groups by sale price, the cheapest tenth sits at a median ratio of 0.982 and the priciest tenth at 0.907, and the decline is nearly monotonic in between. In plain terms a $300,000 home is assessed at about 98 percent of what it sold for while a $1,000,000 home is assessed at about 91 percent. The gap is real and visible, and it is also inside the tolerance the profession allows. Both of those statements are true, and the page says both.

What the dollar figure means

The difference shown on a home is in assessed dollars, not tax dollars. It is the gap between what the county assessed the home at and what it would have been assessed at under the county's own median ratio. Fair Share does not apply a tax rate, because the rate depends on municipality and special districts and quoting a single bill would be a guess.

Tracts

A census tract is shaded only when at least fifteen qualifying 2024 sales fall inside it. 208 of Wake's 230 tracts clear that bar. The rest are drawn faintly and labelled as too few sales rather than coloured as if they had been measured.

The spoken assistant

Click Talk and ask out loud. The browser listens until you pause, Gemini answers from the figures assembled for the selected home and the county study, and ElevenLabs reads the reply. It then listens again so a follow-up such as "compared to my neighbors?" still refers to the same house. Stop ends the conversation. The model is instructed not to invent a tax rate, a tax bill, an appeal deadline, or any number that is not in the data block. When that call fails, the same facts are written out directly.

If you bought here

A buyer does not get a fresh assessment. They inherit the number already on the roll until the next countywide revaluation, which is 2028. The budget box is not a listing search and not a price forecast. It only answers: among 2024 sales at or under that budget, how heavy a ratio did those buyers step into, and in which tracts was it heaviest.

What this cannot say

A high ratio is evidence worth checking, not proof an appeal will succeed. The county's own appraisal may account for condition, renovations, or site features that a sale price and a square footage do not capture. This page cannot see inside a house.
